**Monitoring** is collecting data about a running system's health and behavior — and, critically, alerting a human when that data indicates a problem — so an outage or degradation is caught by the system itself rather than discovered from user complaints.

These four (latency, errors, traffic, and saturation) are commonly called the "four golden signals" — a widely used starting point for what to instrument on any service, regardless of what it actually does.

## Metrics, logs, and traces

- **Metrics** — numeric measurements over time (requests per second, memory usage), cheap to store and graph, good for spotting trends and triggering alerts on thresholds.
- **[Logs](/en/backend/logging)** — discrete, detailed records of specific events, good for understanding exactly what happened during one particular incident.
- **Traces** — the path a single request took across multiple services, showing exactly where time was spent — essential once a request might touch a dozen different internal services before returning.

These three together are often called "observability" — not just knowing *that* something is wrong (monitoring's job) but having enough detail to figure out *why*, which usually requires all three working together.

## Alerting, and alert fatigue

An alert that fires for something that doesn't actually need immediate human attention trains people to ignore alerts generally — a real and common failure mode called **alert fatigue**. Good alerting is tied to something that genuinely requires action (an error rate exceeding a threshold that indicates real user impact) rather than every anomaly a metric could theoretically show, and routes to whoever can actually act on it rather than blasting an entire team for every minor blip.

## SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs

A **Service Level Indicator** is a specific measured value (e.g. "99.95% of requests succeed"); a **Service Level Objective** is the internal target for that indicator; a **Service Level Agreement** is an external, often contractual, commitment to customers — usually a looser target than the internal SLO, leaving a margin so an SLO miss doesn't automatically become a broken customer promise.
