**NAT (Network Address Translation)** rewrites the source address of outgoing packets from a private [IPv4](/en/networking/ipv4) address to a router's public one, and rewrites it back on the way home. It's the reason a home network with a dozen private-range devices only ever needs one public IP — and, historically, one of the biggest reasons IPv4 address exhaustion didn't become a crisis decades sooner.

## How PAT actually distinguishes devices

Since every device shares the router's single public IP, the router has to tell responses apart some other way — it does this by rewriting the **source port** too, and keeping a translation table:

```text
Private side                Public side
192.168.1.10:51000   <-->   203.0.113.5:40001
192.168.1.11:51000   <-->   203.0.113.5:40002
```

Two internal devices can use the identical private port (`51000`) simultaneously, because the router maps each to a distinct public port. A reply arriving at `203.0.113.5:40002` gets translated back to `192.168.1.11:51000` by looking up that table entry — this is exactly the same `(IP, port)` pairing idea behind a [socket](/en/web-fundamentals/ports-sockets).

## What NAT breaks

Because an external host only ever sees the router's public address, it can't initiate a connection *into* a NAT'd device unless the router is explicitly configured to forward a port there. This is by accident a form of firewalling — but it's also why peer-to-peer protocols (VoIP, some multiplayer games) need extra techniques (STUN, TURN, hole punching) just to establish a direct connection through NAT at all.
