Every router makes the same decision for every packet: given this destination address, which of my directly connected links gets it one hop closer? That decision comes from a **routing table** — a list of destination networks and the "next hop" to reach each one. No router needs to know the entire path; it only needs to know the next step, and trust the router after it to know the step after that.

## A routing table, roughly

```text
Destination        Next Hop         Interface
192.168.1.0/24      directly connected  eth0
10.0.0.0/8          10.0.0.1            eth1
0.0.0.0/0           203.0.113.1         eth2   (default route)
```

The `0.0.0.0/0` entry is the **default route** — "anywhere not otherwise listed goes here" — which is why a home router only needs one entry to send all outbound internet traffic to your ISP, without knowing anything about the internet's actual structure.

## Static vs. dynamic routing

## Why the internet doesn't have one giant routing table

No router holds a complete map of every device on Earth — that would be an unworkable amount of state. Instead, [network addressing](/en/networking/network-addressing)'s hierarchical structure lets routers aggregate: a single BGP entry can represent millions of addresses behind one ISP, and a router only needs detail about networks it's actually close to. This is the same principle a postal system uses — a local post office doesn't need to know which street a house is on in another country, only which country's mail center to forward it to.
