**IPv6** addresses are 128 bits — written as eight groups of four hex digits separated by colons (`2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334`), usually shortened by dropping leading zeros in each group and collapsing one run of all-zero groups to `::`. 128 bits isn't just "bigger than 32" — it's roughly 340 undecillion addresses, enough that address scarcity, and the [NAT](/en/networking/nat) it forced on [IPv4](/en/networking/ipv4), stops being a design constraint at all.

## What changed on purpose

IPv6 dropped the IPv4 header checksum (redundant with checksums at the link and transport layers) and NAT-driven address translation is no longer structurally necessary, since every device can have a real, globally routable address. Address configuration can also happen without [DHCP](/en/networking/dhcp) at all: **SLAAC** (Stateless Address Autoconfiguration) lets a device derive its own address from a router advertisement and its network interface identifier.

## Adoption is still partial

IPv6 and IPv4 are not wire-compatible — a device needs a stack for each, and most of the internet runs **dual-stack**, capable of both, falling back to IPv4 wherever IPv6 isn't yet supported end-to-end. This is why IPv4 addresses are still very much alive decades after IPv6 shipped: the transition is additive, not a cutover.
