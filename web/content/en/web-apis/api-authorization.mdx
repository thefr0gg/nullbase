[API Authentication](/en/web-apis/api-authentication) answers "who is this?" Authorization answers the next question: **what is this identity allowed to do?** A request can be perfectly authenticated — a valid, unexpired token — and still get rejected, because the identity behind that token isn't permitted to do what it's asking for. That's the difference between a `401 Unauthorized` (who are you? — actually means "not authenticated," a historically confusing name) and a `403 Forbidden` (I know who you are, and no).

RBAC is the simplest and most common starting point — most APIs that need any authorization at all start there and only reach for ABAC once role-based rules genuinely can't express a needed policy (RBAC answers "what role are you," ABAC can additionally ask "is this specifically your data").

## Where the check happens

Authorization can be enforced at more than one layer, and production systems often use several at once: at the edge/gateway (a scope check before the request even reaches application code), in the application (an RBAC/ABAC check against business logic), and at the data layer (a database row-level policy as a last line of defense if the application check has a bug). The gateway check is cheap and fails fast; the data-layer check is the one that actually can't be bypassed by an application bug.
